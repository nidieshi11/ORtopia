Fleet, control sequences, and admissible insertions

The service region is an influence area A, with a service network of length D. The vehicle set is V , of size F. Service requests arrive in real time, at a rate denoted by μ. Vehicle indices are j ∈ V , written j = 1,…,F in the formulas. At an event instant k, the known previous sequence set is S(k − 1), and the routing decision is

u(k) = S(k) = {S1(k),…,Sj(k),…,SF (k)}. 		(3)

For an individual vehicle,

uj(k) = Sj(k) =[sj1(k)⋯s ji(k)⋯s jwj(k)(k)]T ,

where sji(k) represents the ith stop and wj(k) is the number of stops. The detailed representation of that sequence is

Sj(k) = [ rj1(k) 1 − rj1(k) Γj1(k) labelj1(k) ⋮ ⋮ ⋮ ⋮ rji(k) 1 − rji(k) Γji(k) labelji(k) ⋮ ⋮ ⋮ ⋮ rjwj(k)(k) 1 − rjwj(k)(k) Γjwj(k)(k) labeljwj(k)(k) ]wj(k)×4. 		(7)

For i = 1,…,wj(k),

rji(k) = { 1,if stop i belonging to Sj(k) is a pickup, 0,otherwise.

The first two columns identify a pickup by [10] and a delivery by [01]. The external travel-time quantity Γji(k) is the total travel time between points i − 1 and i, plus the transfer-operation delay at node i. The passenger identifier labelji(k) is used to check precedence: the pickup must occur before the delivery of the same client. No particular analytic travel-time function is specified in this model. Constant vehicle speed is an assumption used for simulation, not an additional general constraint imposed here.

The selected insertion design does not swap previously scheduled stops. The new request contributes a pickup and a delivery to the sequence of the vehicle receiving it. The paper describes the resulting matrix as containing the wj(k − 1) previously scheduled rows and the two new rows. Insertion positions are denoted by

𝜗 = (pu,de),pu ∈{1,…,wj(k) − 1},de ∈{2,…,wj(k)},pu < de.

Here pu and de are integer row positions, not the continuous particles used by the solver. The puth and deth rows of the new sequence are the pickup and delivery of the incoming request. These conditions apply to insertions for the current call and to insertions of each predicted call into the corresponding future sequence. The explicit feasible-pair test in the paper’s procedure is

If pul < del,then 𝜗l = (pul,del).

The subscript l in this last source relation indexes a candidate insertion; it is not a passenger or vehicle index. Particle generation, rounding, repair, and particle-motion equations are solver operations, not additional constraints of (15).

Vehicles follow S(k − 1) until the new call triggers a routing decision. A chosen Sj(k) remains fixed during (k,k + 1) except that reaching a scheduled pickup or delivery removes the accomplished stop. The source describes deletion of the first sequence row when its task is satisfied. Thus insertion expands, and completed service reduces, the sequence and state dimensions. The two-row expansion concerns the vehicle receiving the new request, rather than adding that request to every vehicle.

At instant k, the state refers to S(k − 1), before insertion of the new call. Lji(k) and Tji(k) are, respectively, the vehicle load and clock time of departure after vehicle j leaves stop i, computed at instant k. Vehicle costs and the selected two-step horizon

The selected prediction horizon is N = 2. The controller computes the current and future sequence decisions and applies only S(k), following the receding-horizon rule. Future sequences Sj(k + 1) depend on the predicted request h and the preceding sequence Sj(k); the conditioning bars in the cost expressions retain this dependence without renaming the source variables.

The vehicle-cost definition inherited from the parent objective is

Cj(k + t)|Sj(k+t−2),h = ∑ i=1wj(k+t−1){[L^ ji−1(k + t) + 1](T^ ji(k + t) −T^ ji−1(k + t))⏟ Jtraveltime + rji(k + t − 1)α(T^ ji(k + t) − T j0(k + t))⏟ Jwaitingtime}|Sj(k+t−2),h. 		(11)

Here j = 1,…,F, and for the selected horizon t = 1,2 and h = 1,…,H(k + t). The printed second-step specialization is

Cj(k + 2)|Sj(k),h = ∑ i=1wj(k+1){[L^ ji−1(k + 2) + 1](T^ ji(k + 2) −T^ ji−1(k + 2))⏟ Jtraveltime + rji(k + 1)α(T^ ji(k + 2) − T j0(k + 2))⏟ Jwaitingtime}|Sj(k),h. 		(16)

The expected load L^ji−1(k + t) is the passenger load on segment (i − 1,i). The difference of consecutive expected departure times includes travel along that segment and transfer delay at node i. The added 1 in the travel-time term accounts for the driver and serves as a proxy for operating cost, including empty travel. The waiting-time difference is measured from the current-position time Tj0(k + t), which is printed without a hat. The coefficient α weights waiting time; the general formulation supplies no numerical value or sign restriction for it. The simulation choice α = 1 is not imposed here.

Request probabilities and prediction time

The event step has variable duration: it is the interval between consecutive calls. The source defines k + t as the instant at which the tth request enters, measured from time interval k. The notation ΔT(k + t) identifies the time interval to which event step k + t belongs; it is not defined as an algebraic difference of the vehicle departure-time vectors.

H(k + t) is the number of probable request types at that instant. A type h is an origin–destination pair of clusters, with

h ∈{1,…,H(k + t)}.

The current new request is known. The first-step and second-step probability conditions used in (15) are

H(k + 1) = 1,p1ΔT(k+1)(k + 1) = 1,∑ h=1H(k+2)p hΔT(k+2)(k + 2) = 1.

For the historical-data version used in this paper, the study area is partitioned into clusters. The call mass center is the geographical location of the most likely call during a specified period and within a specified area. The probability construction is

phΔT(k+t) = NhΔT(k+t) ∑ g=1H(k+t)N gΔT(k+t). 		(12)

Here NhΔT(k+t) is the total number of travel requests for origin–destination cluster pair h during the specified interval, from the data used to construct the probabilities. These counts are fixed data, not the prediction horizon N. The source also states

∑ h=1H(k+t)p hΔT(k+t) = 1.

Equation (15) writes these probability parameters with an additional event argument, phΔT(k+t)(k + t); (12) omits that argument. The argument is retained at its printed use sites. No zero-denominator convention is given for (12).

The two-step prediction-time parameter is τ. Physically it represents the expected time for a predicted request to occur, but the authors emphasize its role as the best instant for inserting the future expected call to optimize the routing scheme. It is a continuous tuning parameter calibrated by sensitivity analysis, rather than a routing decision in (15). No general numerical value, bound, or equation connecting τ to Tj0 is printed. The example-specific calibrated values and demand distributions are not conditions of this general formulation.

Printed objective-chain inconsistency

For reference to the selected objective’s derivation, the parent incremental-cost expression is

Min ⁡ S(k)J = ∑ t=1N ∑ j=1F ∑ h=1H(k+t)p hΔT(k+t) ( (C j(k + t) − Cj(k + t − 1))|Sj(k+t−2),h) ,N = 2 here. 		(10)

The original page image of (15), however, prints the following first-line expression:

∑ t=12 ∑ j=1F ∑ h=1H(k+t)p hΔT(k+t)(k + t) ⋅ (C j(k + t) − Cj(k + t + 1))|Sj(k+t−2),h.

The printed k + t + 1 contradicts both (10) and the subsequent expansion in (15). The latter expansion subtracts Cj(k), cancels the first-step cost using the second-step probabilities summing to one, and ends with the selected objective at the start of this article. The conflicting first line is recorded only as a source inconsistency; it is not an additional objective or constraint.
