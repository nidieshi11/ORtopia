Source-defined construction of R^J and R^C

The paper discretizes queue length by discretizing the vehicle arrival rate QA(t,ω) according to FQA(t,φ), that is, by using discrete numbers of vehicles. Applying this discretization to the continuous stochastic regions RJ(t,ω) and RC(t,ω) gives, respectively, R^J(t,ω) and R^C(t,ω). The introducing remark to (33) specifies that these spatially discretized regions impose finitely many nonlinear constraints through fixed probability levels for every prediction step.

The accompanying sampling discussion describes independent instances

ω(1),ω(2),…,ω(M) ∈Ω,distributed according to ℙ,

and discusses removing m constraints. It explains the discretization, but is not a second optimization problem imposed here. The supplied main paper gives no explicit grid, quantile formula, prescribed values of the fixed probability levels, or rule selecting M and m for (33)–(38). Those quantities are not filled in. In particular, no additional inequality of the form of a probability lower bound is appended to the printed subject-to block.

Traffic inputs and premises inherited from Section 2

The selected traffic-input version is the paper’s independent-inflow, static-signal version. It assumes no queue spillover and does not use the optional network extension of Appendix A. For each modeled intersection, ll is the known traffic-light location. The stochastic regions and waves use an elementary event ω in the probability space

[Ω,A, ℙ],ω ∈Ω,

where Ω is the sample space, A is a sigma-algebra, and ℙ is the probability measure. This sample space Ω is distinct in role from the objective coefficient vector Ω in (28) and (33).

The known mean inflow and known CDF are

QA(t) = 𝔼[QA(t,ω)],FQA(t,φ) = ℙ{ω : QA(t,ω) ≤ φ}.

Assumption 1 describes truncation at zero flow and peak capacity flow. The stronger explicit restriction in Section 2.4 is that the flow must be greater than zero and smaller than QC:

0 < QA(t,ω) < QC.

Simple independent distributions are used in the selected version; Poisson and binomial arrivals are possible choices rather than mandatory distributions. Hourly and cycle-averaged traffic flows need not be proportional; ergodicity is not assumed. The randomness in the cycle inflow is represented by FQA.

The link fundamental diagram is assumed known in mean value and triangular. Its defining points are actual flow [ρA(t,ω),QA(t,ω)], peak capacity [ρC,QC], and jam [ρJ,0]. Flow is assumed constant over short intervals; a time-independent QA(ω) produces linear shockwave profiles and polygonal regions. The traffic model is discretized in passenger car equivalents (PCE). The network layout and bus frequency are assumed such that buses have only a minor effect on the link fundamental diagram. Velocity control is intended where the bus is sufficiently far from the intersection and traffic is not too dense, or overtaking space exists.

The three physical traffic states are free flow at desired vehicle speed vA(t), jam RJ(t,ω) at density ρJ and

vJ = 0,

and saturated queue discharge RC(t,ω) at critical velocity vC. Outside the two constrained regions, vehicles travel at their desired speed vA(t). The paper leaves the critical velocity as traffic data rather than providing an extra formula for it here.

The signal program must be known and frozen over the MPC prediction horizon. With cycle counter c, red-phase start t1,c, red-phase end t2,c, and cycle time tcyc,c, the defining relations are

tred,c = t2,c − t1,c,tgreen,c = tcyc,c − tred,c,tcyc,c = tred,c + tgreen,c.

For the static timing used in the deductions,

tgreen = tcyc − (t2 − t1).

The notation ctcyc denotes time elapsed since an arbitrary initial instant. The selected version does not replace it with the varying-cycle alternative.
