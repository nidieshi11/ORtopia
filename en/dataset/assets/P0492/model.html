The continuation value is the optimal value of the nonlinear knapsack problem

V ^(x) ≡ max ⁡ kn ∑ n=1NV n∗(x n,kn) s.t. ∑ n=1Nt nkn ≤ MT. 		(5)

Here kn is the number of vehicle trips made available to customer n, hence a nonnegative integer count. The printed block (5) contains no separate domain row or individual upper bound for kn. The fixed quantity tn is the time required for a vehicle route from the supplier to customer n and back; M is the number of vehicles; and T is the maximum work time per vehicle per time period. The knapsack constructs a value, not the feasible action set in (6): the paper explicitly qualifies the resulting assignment as a possible policy only “if feasible for the IRPDD.”

Inherited inputs, states, and feasible itineraries

There is one product, one supplier’s plant, and N customers indexed by n = 1,…,N. The fleet has M homogeneous vehicles of known capacity CV . Customer n has known storage capacity Cn. Time is discrete, t = 0,1,…, and a period is called a day. The inventory state is

X = [0,C1] × [0,C2] ×⋯ × [0,CN],Xnt ∈ [0,Cn],Xt = (X1t,…,XNt) ∈X.

For the decomposition in Section 6.1 the paper assumes that the demand distribution and thus the state space X are discrete. It does not specify a general discretization grid here.

The fixed joint distribution F of customer demand is known to the supplier and does not change with time. Demand vectors on different days are independent; independence between customers on the same day is not assumed. Write Unt for customer n’s demand on day t, Fn for the marginal distribution of Un, and fn(un) for the probability that customer n’s demand is un in the discrete version. The discount factor is

α ∈ [0,1).

The supplier can measure the customers’ inventory levels at any time. An itinerary has duration less than one day, so all vehicles and drivers are available at the beginning of each day. A vehicle may perform more than one route per day.

For the direct-delivery specialization, A(x) is the set of itineraries whose routes each visit only one customer and that satisfy the inherited workload, travel-time and driver-work-hour restrictions, customer delivery time windows, vehicle capacities, customer storage capacities after delivery, and other application-specific itinerary restrictions. These restrictions are specified as part of the itinerary set in the paper, not as a separate algebraic routing formulation.

An itinerary a consists of individual-customer itineraries an, n = 1,…,N. Each an specifies the number of visits to customer n by each vehicle and the amount delivered by each vehicle. The quantity dn(a), or dn(an) in the direct-delivery notation, is the total amount delivered to customer n; kn(an) is the number of times that customer is visited by a vehicle. The function kn(an) counts actual visits, whereas the state/knapsack variable kn is the number of trips made available.

The inherited feasibility requirements include

At ∈A(Xt)(t = 0,1,…),Xnt + dn(At) ≤ Cn(n = 1,…,N,t = 0,1,…).

The latter is the version actually used by the authors, assuming that no product is used between the inventory measurement and delivery. Vehicle capacities must not be exceeded on any route. The aggregate workload inequality in (5) does not replace these itinerary restrictions.

Every action At must depend only on the history

(X0,A0,X1,…,Xt),

not on future information. A stationary deterministic policy π ∈Π chooses π(x) ∈A(x) using only the current state x. Its customer-n itinerary is denoted by πn(x).

Inventory dynamics and system reward

Demand not satisfied is lost, not backlogged. For every customer and day the amount used, shortage, and next inventory are, respectively,

min ⁡ {Xnt + dn(At),Unt},
Snt = max ⁡ {Unt − (Xnt + dn(At)),0},Xn,t+1 = max ⁡ {Xnt + dn(At) − Unt,0}.

For x ∈X, a ∈A(x), and a measurable B ⊆X, the paper defines

U(x,a,B) ≡ {U ∈ ℝ+N : max ⁡ {x n + dn(a) − Un,0}∈ B},
P[Xt+1 ∈ B∣Xt = x,At = a] = Q[B∣x,a] ≡ F[U(x,a,B)].

The defining display for U is retained in its printed component notation, although B is a subset of the vector state space.

The fixed functions rn(dn), hn(⋅), and pn(sn) are, respectively, the reward for a delivery, the inventory holding cost, and the penalty for unsatisfied demand. They remain abstract functions. The fixed cn is the transportation cost for making a delivery at customer n. The direct-delivery expected single-stage system reward used in (6) is

g(x,a) ≡∑ n=1N{r n(dn(an)) − cnkn(an) − hn(xn + dn(an)) − EFn [pn (max ⁡ {Un − (xn + dn(an)),0})]}. 		(3)

Here EFn denotes expectation with respect to the marginal demand distribution Fn.

Individual-customer transition construction

Fix a policy π ∈Π and assume the existence of unique stationary probabilities νπ(x) under that policy. The conditional probability that the next inventory at customer n is yn and that the next policy itinerary visits that customer kn times, given present inventory xn and delivery dn, is

qn(yn,kn∣xn,dn) = ∑ {s∈X:sn=xn,dn(πn(s))=dn}νπ(s)∑ {z∈X:zn=yn,kn(πn(z))=kn}Q[z∣s,π(s)] ∑ {s∈X:sn=xn,dn(πn(s))=dn}νπ(s) . 		(4)

This expression applies if the denominator is positive. The printed exceptional case is

qn(yn,kn∣xn,dn) = 0if the denominator in (4) is zero.

No alternative probability distribution is supplied for that case.

For each n = 1,…,N, the customer MDP has state (xn,kn): xn is the customer’s inventory and up to kn vehicle visits are available. Inventory coordinates xn,yn belong to the corresponding discrete inventory state space within [0,Cn]; kn,ln are visit counts. The admissible set A(xn,kn) consists of dispatching up to kn vehicle trips to customer n, with delivered amounts constrained by vehicle capacity CV and customer storage capacity Cn.

Its printed transition relation is

P[(Xn,t+1,Kn,t+1) = (yn,ln)∣(Xnt,Knt) = (xn,kn),Ant = an] = qn(yn,ln∣xn,dn(an)).

The single-stage reward specified in Section 6.1 is

gn(xn,an) ≡ rn(dn(an)) − cnkn(an) − hn(xn) − EFn [pn (max ⁡ {Un − (xn + dn(an)),0})].

Its optimal discounted value is

V n∗(x n,kn) ≡ sup ⁡ {Ant}t=0∞E [ ∑ t=0∞αtg n(Xnt,Ant)|(Xn0,Kn0) = (xn,kn)],

subject to

Ant ∈A(Xnt,Knt),n = 1,…,N,t = 0,1,…,

and the requirement that the actions are nonanticipatory: they use only the customer process history available at the decision time. These are the individual-customer values used in (5).

Printed reward contradiction. Section 6.1, physical page 11, asserts

g(x,a) = ∑ n=1Ng n(xn,an).

However, its definition of gn contains − hn(xn), whereas (3) contains − hn(xn + dn(an)). The equality is therefore not generally consistent with the two printed definitions. Above, (3) is retained for the system reward in (6), and the printed Section 6.1 definition is retained for the customer values. The asserted equality is recorded here as a source contradiction, not imposed as an additional identity or used to change either holding-cost argument.

Selected nonparametric estimation and policy update

The selected version is Algorithm 1, using the reduced conditional probabilities of physical page 14. This uses the following construction in place of the direct five-dimensional empirical update (7); the two estimators are not simultaneously imposed.

Under the fixed simulation policy π, define the probability that customer n is visited by kn vehicles conditional on inventory yn by

pn(kn∣yn) = ∑ {x∈X:xn=yn,kn(πn(x))=kn}νπ(x) ∑ {x∈X:xn=yn}νπ(x) if the denominator is positive.

The zero-denominator sentence is printed with a different customer index:

pi(ki∣yi) = 0if the denominator is zero.

This is retained as the printed exceptional-case notation. The conditional probability pn(kn∣yn) is distinct from the shortage-penalty function pn(sn); the paper uses the same letter for both, distinguished by their arguments.

Let p^nt(kn∣yn) estimate pn(kn∣yn) after t transitions of the simulation, and let Nnt(yn) be the number of times customer n has been in inventory state yn by transition t. The initial-estimate weight is denoted by Nn0(kn∣yn), analogously to the initial observation weight in (7). The printed update is

p^n,t+1(kn∣yn) = { (Nn0(kn∣yn) + Nnt(yn))p^nt(kn∣yn) + 1 Nn0(kn∣yn) + Nnt(yn) + 1 , if Xnt = yn and kn(πn(Xt)) = kn, (Nn0(kn∣yn) + Nnt(yn))p^nt(kn∣yn) Nn0(kn∣yn) + Nnt(yn) + 1 , if Xnt = yn and kn(πn(Xt))≠kn, p^nt(kn∣yn), if Xnt≠yn.

The resulting estimate of the customer transition probability is

The lower endpoint un = xn + dn and the separate boundary case yn = 0 are as printed. The paper notes that these estimates generally differ from those obtained using (7). Equation (4) defines the conditional probabilities being approximated; it is not an extra requirement that the reduced estimates equal that stationary-probability ratio. In the updated customer MDPs, the estimated transition probabilities replace the exact qn in the transition relation above.

The paper states that the p^ estimates have convergence results similar to the direct empirical estimates: for states occurring infinitely often, the estimates converge with probability one under the fixed simulation policy. No numerical initial weights, estimates, or stopping tolerances are prescribed for this construction.

Algorithm 1: policy-update construction.

  1. Start with an initial policy π^0 and set i = 0.

  2. Repeat the following simulation, solution, policy update, and increment for a chosen number of iterations or until a convergence test is satisfied.

  3. Simulate the IRPDD under π^i to estimate pn(kn∣yn) using the selected updates above.

  4. With the updated estimates, formulate and solve the updated individual-customer MDPs, obtaining V n∗(xn,kn).

  5. Define π^i+1 by (6), with V ^ given by (5) using those updated customer values.

  6. Increment i ← i + 1.
