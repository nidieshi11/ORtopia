Exp = min ⁡ r {r ⋅ 𝔼 [min ⁡ (q,D(z,𝜖))]} s.t. 𝔼 [min ⁡ (q,D(z,𝜖))] ≥ Γ, r ≥ 0. 		(2)

The government anticipates the supplier’s optimal price and production decisions for the announced consumer subsidy r. The supplier solves

Π = max ⁡ q,p {p ⋅ 𝔼 [min ⁡ (q,D(z,𝜖))] − c ⋅ q}. 		(1)

Decision roles and operative definitions

This is a single-period, full-information, two-stage Stackelberg game with one supplier. The government is the leader and chooses the subsidy per product, r, paid directly to the end consumer. The supplier observes r and chooses the selling price p (the manufacturer’s suggested retail price) and production quantity q. Demand is then realized and sales occur. The effective consumer price and realized sales are, respectively,

z = p − r,min ⁡ (q,D(z,𝜖)).

The fixed adoption target Γ is common knowledge. The government must attain it in expectation. The fixed marginal unit cost c may combine manufacturing cost and the cost of an additional unit of manufacturing capacity. When no demand information is gained between capacity construction and production, capacity is built according to planned production. The selected model sets salvage value v and the underage penalty u to

v = u = 0.

The symbol Π in (1) denotes the supplier’s optimal expected profit. The symbol Exp in (2) denotes minimum expected subsidy expenditure. Section 3.2 also writes expected expenditure, before minimization, as

Exp = r ⋅ 𝔼 [min ⁡ (q,D(z,𝜖))]. 		(5)

Thus the paper uses Exp both for the expenditure expression and for its optimal value.

The supplier’s response notation is p∗(r) for optimal price and q∗(p,r) for optimal quantity conditional on price and subsidy. The p and q appearing in the government’s problem are these responses:

p = p∗(r),q = q∗(p∗(r),r).

They are not additional choices of the government.

Selected demand version: general additive uncertainty

The selected version is Section 4.1, with the nominal demand function left abstract. Demand is

D(z,𝜖) = y(z) + 𝜖. 		(8)

Here

y(z) = 𝔼[D(z,𝜖)],z = p − r,

and 𝜖 is a random variable with cumulative distribution function F𝜖. Expectations are over this demand uncertainty. Demand represents aggregate demand over the entire single-period horizon.

Assumption 1 imposes the following input conditions:

  • Demand depends on p and r only through their difference z.
  • The deterministic demand function y(z) is positive, twice differentiable, and decreasing in z, and hence invertible.

  • At p = c and r = 0, the adoption target cannot be achieved:

    y(c) < Γ.

  • The additive noise has zero mean:

    𝔼[𝜖] = 0.

No linear or isoelastic specialization, particular noise distribution, support endpoints, or numerical instance is selected.

Linked supplier-response and government-solution characterization

For the additive version, Theorem 1 assumes

2y′(z) + (p − c) ⋅ y″(z) + c2 p3 ⋅ 1 f𝜖 (F𝜖−1 ((p − c)∕p)) < 0. 		(9)

Here f𝜖 is the noise density and F𝜖−1 is the inverse CDF appearing in the paper’s newsvendor quantile. The paper states that (9) guarantees uniqueness of optimal price as a function of the subsidy and assumes it for the remainder of the analysis. It prints no explicit price or effective-price interval with (9); none is added here.

Under Assumption 1 and (9), the optimal supplier price for a given r solves

y(p − r) + 𝔼 [min ⁡ (F𝜖−1 (p − c p ),𝜖)] + y′(p − r) ⋅ (p − c) = 0. 		(10)

Using that price, the optimal production quantity is

q∗(p,r) = y(p − r) + F 𝜖−1 (p − c p ). 		(11)

Theorem 1 further states that the government’s adoption constraint is tight at optimality. With the paper’s stochastic-optimum notation,

psto = p∗(r sto),qsto = q∗(p sto,rsto),zsto = psto − rsto,

this statement is

𝔼 [min ⁡ (qsto,D(zsto,𝜖))] = Γ.

Equations (10)–(11) and this optimality equality characterize the solution of (1)–(2); they are not additional feasibility restrictions imposed on arbitrary decisions. The paper does not supersede (1)–(2) with another decentralized optimization block in this general additive version.

Printed scope and domains

The explicit subsidy restriction is r ≥ 0. Equation (1) prints maximization over q,p without an explicit feasible-set block, interval bounds, or integrality declarations. These are retained as printed rather than supplemented with invented bounds. In particular, p ≥ c is described later as a consequence of decentralized optimality, not as an additional constraint in (1). No price cap, subsidy cap, effective-price bound, demand truncation, or noise-support restriction is introduced here.
